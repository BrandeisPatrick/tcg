# Mechanics survey — keep builds certain, spend luck before turn one

Written 2026-11-04 from a seven-part research pass over about forty card,
auto-battler, roguelike and MOBA-derived games. Companion to
[playability.md](./playability.md), the plan it tests; "§" below refers to
that document's sections.

The precedents back the core of the redesign in [playability.md](./playability.md): souls that accumulate, lumpy item tiers, spells turned into cooldown actives and a single announced objective are all things shipped games did, and **no surveyed respawning-hero game strips a hero's gear when it dies**. The same precedents show the plan will miss its own gates unless four things change. **Time is the binding constraint**: the engine counts both players' turns, so the 10–14-turn length gate gives each player only 5–7 turns and 20–28 lane souls, too few for an 18-soul T4 or for "save three turns for a T3" to be one choice among several. **The 66% first-player edge is structural**: it exceeds Hearthstone's pre-Coin alpha (about 60/40), it grows from refill tempo and free extra swings, and the plan would add to it, because the human seat also drafts first and the first scheduled boss opens on the first player's turn; Riot's October 2026 admission that an extra rune did not fix Riftbound's seat edge says a two-soul head start will not fix this one alone. **Several Deadlock facts are stale and the price budget is too flat**: the item ladder has been 1 : 2 : 4 : 8 since May 2025, the Rejuvenator is three stealable revive credits rather than a mass resurrection, respawn and comeback both scale, and the one published per-effect cost regression prices a heal at about 0.4 of a damage point. **The 300-game sim cannot test the gates it sets**; they need roughly 1,000–2,500 games a pass. On the shop, the evidence favors A over B, but A as written goes beyond every card-game precedent, and the design the literature converges on is a third: a market fixed before turn one, which keeps every build executable while making each match different.

## 1. Five families of precedent, and what each one bought

The research covered about forty games across physical TCGs and LCGs, digital card games, auto-battlers, roguelike deckbuilders and MOBA-derived card and board games, plus the design writing around them. Each family below states rules and numbers first, then what the mechanics did for pacing, fairness, decision depth and MOBA feel. A table at the end condenses the section.

### Physical TCGs and LCGs turn paying into a decision and cap the first turn

Physical card games mostly turn paying into a decision. Magic allows one land a turn ([Wikipedia: MTG rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering_rules)) and Lorcana one card into the inkwell ([RulesPal: Lorcana](https://rulespal.com/disney-lorcana/rulebook)). Flesh and Blood pays costs by pitching cards from hand for **1, 2 or 3 resource points** and discards whatever is unspent at the end of the turn ([Star City Games](https://articles.starcitygames.com/flesh-and-blood/how-to-play-flesh-and-blood-2/)); Final Fantasy TCG changed its rule in **August 2026** so that unused CP simply ceases to exist, because payment rules that depended on how CP was produced had become too complicated ([Square Enix](https://fftcg.square-enix-games.com/en/news/changes-to-rules-for-paying-cp)). Riftbound, Riot's League of Legends TCG, keeps resources in a separate **12-card rune deck**: a player channels two runes a turn and either exhausts a rune for generic energy or recycles it for colored power ([Riot](https://playriftbound.com/en-us/news/organizedplay/testing-start-of-game-rules/)). Digimon shares a single **memory gauge** between both players, running from 10 on one side through 0 to 10 on the other: paying a cost slides the marker toward the opponent, the turn ends once it rests on their side, and passing hands them exactly 3 ([Digimon Comprehensive Rules v4.3](https://en.digimoncard.com/rule/pdf/general_rule.pdf?260619)). Reviewers praise the gauge because each turn becomes a question of spending just enough ([Penny Arcade](https://automata.penny-arcade.com/news/post/2021/08/02/digiman)) and fault it for confusing newcomers *(excerpt)* ([Board Game Guys](https://boardgameguys.com/?p=61668)). Pokémon commits energy to a unit, one attachment a turn and lost on knockout, while Battle Spirits moves the cores a player loses from life into that player's own reserve, a refund built into the rules ([Wikipedia: Pokémon TCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Trading_Card_Game); [Wikipedia: Battle Spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_Spirits)).

Nearly every one of these games also restricts the first turn or compensates the second seat. Magic and Lorcana skip the first player's draw, Pokémon's first player can neither attack nor play a Supporter, Yu-Gi-Oh's neither draws nor attacks, and KeyForge's may play or discard only one card ([Wikipedia: MTG rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering_rules); [RulesPal: Lorcana](https://rulespal.com/disney-lorcana/rulebook); [Wikipedia: Pokémon TCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Trading_Card_Game); [Wikipedia: Yu-Gi-Oh! TCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu-Gi-Oh!_Trading_Card_Game); [Archon Arcana](https://archonarcana.com/First_Turn_Rule)). With such rules in place, 17Lands data put Magic limited's gap between playing and drawing first at **about 3 points** (roughly 51.5% to 48.5%), **6** in the fastest format tracked, while slow formats favored the player on the draw ([MTGAZone](https://mtgazone.com/?p=141724)). Riftbound's second player channels a third rune on its first turn; because the rune deck holds only twelve, that lead lasts five turns ([GamerTag Mythras](https://gamertagmythras.com/blog/riftbound-tcg/riftbound-going-second-rules-test)). In **October 2026** Riot said going first still won more often than it wanted and began testing a starting point, an extra card or a pre-draw prediction for the second player ([Riot](https://playriftbound.com/en-us/news/organizedplay/testing-start-of-game-rules/)).

The family also shows how to guarantee identity and progression. Flesh and Blood starts the hero, its weapon and its equipment in play, never shuffles them into the deck, and limits the deck to the hero's class ([FaB Comprehensive Rules §1](https://rules.fabtcg.com/en/cr/01-game-concepts/)). Grand Archive levels a champion only by materializing exactly the next level from a separate material deck, so the path can never be drawn badly ([Grand Archive rules](https://rules.gatcg.com/general-rules/general-rules-card-types/card-types-champion)), and Vanguard's four-card ride deck does the same *(excerpt)* ([Bushiroad](https://en.cf-vanguard.com/howto/d-series/)). Win conditions that count up put the score on the board: Lorcana's 20 lore, Netrunner's 7 agenda points, KeyForge's three keys at 6 Æmber with a mandatory warning when a key can be forged next turn, and Smash Up bases that pay the top three players when their power breakpoint is reached ([Lorcana Comprehensive Rules](https://files.disneylorcana.com/Disney%20Lorcana%20Comprehensive%20Rules%20-%2004.13.24.pdf); [Wikipedia: Android: Netrunner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Android:_Netrunner); [Archon Arcana: Turn](https://www.archonarcana.com/wiki/Turn); [AEG Smash Up rulebook](https://www.alderac.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/SU7_Rulebook.pdf)).

**Inference.** For pacing, one resource drop a turn and first-turn limits cap how fast tempo builds. For fairness, those caps plus explicit compensation hold seat edges to a few points, and Riftbound shows that a resource gift alone can fall short. For decision depth, the richest systems give the resource a second use (ink or play, pitch or play, exhaust or recycle) or a consequence that lands on the opponent (Digimon's overspend). For MOBA feel the family offers little directly, but it supplies the two structures this game most needs: a build that exists from turn one and a level path that cannot be drawn badly.

### Digital card games traded responses for speed, then had to buy back the second seat

Hearthstone's refill ramp (one crystal a turn up to 10, unspent mana lost) handed the first player so much that in alpha the coin-flip winner was about **20% more likely to win** even with the second player holding an extra card ([BlizzPro](https://blizzpro.com/?p=70449); [Hearthstone Wiki: Mana](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Mana)). Ben Brode's arithmetic showed why: in a game that ends on turn 7, the first player has spent **28 mana to the second player's 21**. Starting the second player at +5 health moved the numbers, but going second still felt like losing because that player always had less to spend; the Coin (one temporary crystal, given with a fourth card) brought the first player to **52.2% overall and 50.4% in Master League** by September 2013 ([BlizzPro](https://blizzpro.com/?p=70449)). Later metas swung the going-first edge between **about 1 and 5.75 points**, and aggressive decks widened it: the Odd Paladin mirror went **62%** to the first player ([Vicious Syndicate](https://www.vicioussyndicate.com/?p=14794)). Legends of Runeterra kept refill mana but banks up to **3** unspent mana for spells, a rule its producer says began as a way to soften bad openings and create branching decisions, and it alternates the attack token each round ([Out of Games](https://outof.games/news/501-legends-of-runeterra-developer-interview/); [Wikipedia: Legends of Runeterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legends_of_Runeterra)). Gwent found going first was the disadvantage and gives that player a 5-point boost and an extra mulligan ([TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/gwent-the-witcher-card-game-review)); Shadowverse gives the second player three evolution points to the first player's two, plus an extra card ([Wikipedia: Shadowverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadowverse)).

Marvel Snap cut length and dead time instead: 12-card decks, six turns, three random locations revealed over the first three turns, win two of three, simultaneous turns and about three minutes a game, with stakes that double on a snap and automatically on the final turn ([Wikipedia: Marvel Snap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvel_Snap); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/ben-brode-bets-super-speed-will-make-marvel-snap-stand-out/)); its six turns came from testing five, six and seven *(excerpt)* ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/marvel-snap-is-the-first-game-from-former-hearthstone-director-ben-brodes-new-studio)). Hearthstone's team removed responses on the opponent's turn and cut rules that needed explaining, then restored summoning sickness when removing it cost too much depth ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-iterate-fast-and-other-design-lessons-learned-from-i-hearthstone-i-)), and it replaced an instant deck-out loss with escalating fatigue after a playtest in which a nearly won game ended on the spot and made the earlier decisions feel meaningless ([Hearthstone Wiki: Fatigue](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Fatigue)).

Two digital games removed the draw entirely. Hearthstone Mercenaries (2021) fielded three of six mercenaries, each with up to three abilities ordered by speed and limited by cooldowns; any ability with a cooldown starts the game unavailable, and each mercenary's single piece of equipment is chosen before the match ([Hearthstone Wiki: Mercenaries](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Mercenaries)). Its content ended with patch 25.4 in February 2023, and postmortems blame its grind and monetization rather than its in-match kit ([BlizzardWatch](https://blizzardwatch.com/2023/02/07/hearthstone-mercenaries-mode-ends/)). Prismata has no deck at all: both players buy from an **11-unit base set plus 5 or more random units** drawn from about a hundred, fixed for the match, with perfect information afterwards ([Wikipedia: Prismata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prismata)). Its designer, Elyot Grant, reports that balancing took **over three years and more than a dozen restarts**, that early versions had dominant strategies in which one overpowered unit absorbed both players' whole economies, that the seat advantage was once large, and that the team made losing positions end faster instead of adding catch-up ([Game Developer: Removing RNG](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/removing-rng-how-eliminating-luck-can-benefit-strategy-card-games)). It holds an **81% positive** Steam rating from 1,226 reviews ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/490220/Prismata/)) yet ran at a few hundred to a few thousand concurrent players in 2018 ([MMOBomb](https://mmobomb.com/news/small-indie-game-prismata-briefly-steams-4-game-thanks-card-farmers)).

**Inference.** Refill ramps make pacing a curve the first player always leads, which is why refill games ship compensations (the Coin, extra evolution points, a spell bank) and why Hearthstone, the best measured of them, still lands one to five points off even. Decision depth came from what designers added (a bank, stakes, simultaneous turns), not from refill itself. For MOBA feel, Mercenaries proved cooldown kits work on a turn-based board, and Prismata is both the nearest precedent for an always-open shop and the clearest record of what it costs to balance one.

### Auto-battlers made the economy itself the game

Teamfight Tactics pays a flat **5 gold** a round (2 to 4 in the opening rounds), **1 gold of interest per 10 banked** up to 5 at 50, streak gold of **+1 at three or four, +2 at five and +3 at six or more** consecutive wins or losses, and 1 more per win; a reroll of the five-slot shop costs **2** and four experience costs **4**, and power is gated by level-based odds (a 5-cost unit appears **3%** of the time at level 8) and finite pools rather than by price ([LoL Wiki: TFT Gold](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/TFT:Gold); [Metabot](https://metabot.gg/en/TFT/guides/tft-when-to-roll-vs-level-up-shop-odds)). Riot's patch 14.1 moved every streak threshold one step later, so five straight losses pay 6 gold instead of 10, explicitly to hold streak gold back until later in the second stage ([TFT patch 14.1 notes](https://teamfighttactics.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/game-updates/teamfight-tactics-patch-14-1-notes/)); the shared carousel lets the lowest-health players pick first ([LoL Wiki: TFT Carousel](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/TFT:Carousel)). TFT's design lead has pointed out that an eight-player free-for-all lowers each player's stake in any one result, unlike a 50-50 duel ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-teamfight-tactics-i-was-designed-to-target-former-i-league-of-legends-i-players)).

Hearthstone Battlegrounds refills **3 coins rising to 10**, prices every minion at **3**, buys back at **1**, refreshes for **1**, freezes the shop for free, and gates power through tavern upgrades costing **5/7/8/11/11** that each fall by 1 for every round a player waits; hero damage is the winner's tier plus its surviving minions' tiers ([Hearthstone Wiki: Battlegrounds](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Battlegrounds)). Super Auto Pets gives 10 gold a turn with no carry-over and prices everything at 3 ([SAP Wiki: Gold](https://superautopets.wiki.gg/wiki/Gold)). Backpack Battles scripts its gold by round (10 to 22, spiking to 22 when subclasses unlock in round 8), charges 1 for each of the first four rerolls in a round and 2 after, and refunds half on a sale ([Backpack Battles Wiki: Gold](https://backpackbattles.wiki.gg/wiki/Gold)). Mechabellum sells income outright, 100 supply for +50 a round ([Mechabellum Wiki: Supply](https://mechabellum.wiki.gg/wiki/Supply)). Dota Underlords fell from about 111,000 concurrent players in July 2019 to under 15,000 by December and has had no update since November 2020; no source blames its economy ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/i-cant-believe-this-abandoned-valve-game-still-has-players-and-is-fun-4-years-later/)).

**Inference.** Pacing in this family comes from a schedule (levels, tiers, scripted windfalls) rather than from prices. Fairness is easier with eight players to absorb shop luck, and even there Riot had to delay the streak gold that paid early losing. Decision depth comes from income sources that pull against each other (interest rewards holding, streaks reward committing, wins reward strength) and from the roll-or-level choice. MOBA feel is strong for the economy, carries, spikes and comebacks, with no map at all. Two pieces transfer cleanly to a duel: a price that falls while you wait (the tavern discount makes "when" a question without any banking rule) and first access to a shared reward for whoever is behind.

### Roguelike deckbuilders price each purchase in fights of income

Slay the Spire pays **10 to 20 gold** a normal fight, 25 to 35 an elite and 95 to 105 a boss, from a start of 99 ([StS Wiki: Gold](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Gold)), against cards at **45–55, 68–83 and 135–165** by rarity, relics at 143–158, 238–263 and 285–315, one card at half price in each shop, and a card-removal service that starts at **75 and rises by 25** with each use ([StS Wiki: Merchant](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Merchant)). Slay the Spire 2's first major patch cut every shop relic by 25 gold and removed relics that generated large amounts of gold ([FinalBoss](https://finalboss.io/slay-the-spire-2s-first-big-patch-just)); its harder ascension starts removal at 100 and raises it by 50 a use *(excerpt)* ([pulse.bot](https://www.pulse.bot/gaming/news/slay-the-spire-2-nerfs-card-removal-buffs-shivs-and-adds-a-new-reward-system-ae62a358-c372-4e4d-9dfd-cf94ece210ec)). Balatro makes saving the central temptation: **$1 of interest per $5 held**, capped at $5 and raisable to $10 and $20 by vouchers, rerolls that start at $5 and rise $1 each within a shop, and items that sell for half ([Balatro Wiki: Interest](https://balatrowiki.org/w/Interest); [Balatro Wiki: The Shop](https://balatrowiki.org/w/The_Shop)). Its designer balances by feel, says almost every fix changes a number such as cost or rarity, and defines an overpowered card as one that crowds out its neighbors ([Rogueliker](https://rogueliker.com/balatro-interview/)).

**Inference.** At roughly 15 gold a fight, a Slay the Spire common card costs about **3.3 fights**, a rare about 10 and a rare relic about 20, so a shop visit buys one to three things and saving is a plan across several floors. The TCG's 3/6/11/18 at 4 souls a turn costs **0.75, 1.5, 2.75 and 4.5 own turns**, far cheaper than Slay the Spire and close to TFT's units; how many purchases that permits depends on match length, which section 3 shows is shorter than the plan assumes. The family adds two tools: escalating prices on any repeatable service, and interest that taxes every purchase made below the cap. In a duel, interest also compounds whoever is ahead, so the precedents favor opt-in saving that costs a slot, such as Balatro's vouchers, Mechabellum's supply cards or Deadlock's own Golden Goose Egg, an 800-soul item whose value grows while it is held ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/deadlock/update-march-2026-patch-notes)), over a universal interest rule.

### MOBA-derived games kept the economy, the spike and the objective, not the map

Valve's Artifact (2018, designed by Richard Garfield) is the most literal translation: three lanes with **40-HP towers** and an **80-HP Ancient** behind them, per-lane mana that refills, gold of **5 per hero kill and 1 per creep** that carries over, a shopping phase after all three lanes, weapon, armor and accessory slots whose items **stay on a dead hero**, and heroes who sit out a round when killed ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/artifact/artifact-review-pc); [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/games/artifact/b/pc/archive/2018/03/09/how-do-you-play-it-and-how-will-you-pay-for-it); [PCGamesN: How to play Artifact](https://www.pcgamesn.com/artifact/how-to-play-artifact)). Critics scored it 76 on Metacritic, yet concurrent players fell from **about 60,000 to about 1,500 within two months** and Valve halted development in March 2021 ([Wikipedia: Artifact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_(video_game))). Garfield blamed the revenue model, missing short-term goals and review-bombing ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/artifact-designer-says-it-failed-due-to-cards-for-money-1835212145)); an alpha tester's postmortem adds complexity, a steep skill gap and randomness that felt larger than it was ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/6gptwR8jBkzqhWaTY/artifact-what-went-wrong)). The 2.0 rework removed random attack arrows and random deployment, and its reviewer found it more satisfying because losses no longer came from things the player could not help ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/artifact/2-0-review)).

Riftbound has no gold, shop or towers. Players score a point for conquering a battlefield and another each turn they hold it, and win at **8** ([Riot: How to Play](https://playriftbound.com/en-us/news/rules-and-releases/how-to-play-get-started/)); the final point cannot come from a lone conquest unless every battlefield was scored that turn ([riftbound.gg](https://riftbound.gg/the-in-depth-guide-to-scoring-in-riftbound/)). Its director, Dave Guskin, says the first prototypes ported League literally and were abandoned, that the team never wanted a one-to-one translation, and that it chose a score counting up to victory over life totals counting down ([Card Gamer](https://cardgamer.com/features/dave-guskin-riftbound-origins-interview/); [Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/riftbound-league-of-legends-trading-card-game/preview-impressions-interview)). He also says MOBA cooldowns do not carry over to cards, and that a repeatable execute on a unit felt so bad that Darius's execute moved onto a one-shot signature spell ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/league-of-legends/making-a-riftbound-card)). A reviewer found its territory control highly thematic of League ([Card Gamer review](https://cardgamer.com/?p=30622)).

Guards of Atlantis II, which critics call the best tabletop MOBA, abstracts minions into one battle zone resolved by the difference in counts, drops hit points, charges the team respawn tokens on each death (more for a high-level hero; a level-7 hero costs three), spends coins on level-ups that offer two cards with the unchosen one becoming a permanent +1 item, ends each card track in an Ultimate, and offers three ways to win including a wave clock ([Space-Biff](https://spacebiff.com/2023/04/24/guards-of-atlantis-ii); [Way Too Many Games](https://waytoomany.games/2023/05/08/guards-of-atlantis-ii-is-the-best-moba-board-game-yet/); [RulesPal](https://rulespal.com/guards-of-atlantis-tabletop-moba/rulebook)). Cloudspire's creep waves resolve almost entirely on their own, which its reviewer found boring to watch ([Space-Biff](https://spacebiff.com/2019/10/30/cloudspire/)). Heroes of the Storm runs a MOBA with no shop at all, shared team experience and talents chosen at level thresholds ([Wikipedia: Heroes of the Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heroes_of_the_Storm)). A former Riot systems designer describes the genre's loop as intensity that builds across a match, gold that buys the items that break defenses, objectives at regular intervals that pull players out of lane, and leads that snowball while leaving a window to catch up ([Game Design Skills](https://gamedesignskills.com/?p=29764)).

**Inference.** Across these games, reviewers credited the MOBA feel to contested places, objectives that count toward winning, visible level-ups and ultimates, team fights and comebacks, and kill gold turned into recognizable items. They did not credit automated creeps, random targeting, three boards at once or count-down life totals, and Riot found one-to-one simulation a dead end. Because Riftbound and Heroes of the Storm both work without a shop, the shop is this game's Deadlock-specific identity rather than a genre requirement.

## 2. What the precedents imply for the four problems

### No loop: every turn needs a question, and every match needs a clock

§2 says a MOBA turn is earn, buy, spike, fight, take the objective, and that nothing on this board asks a question. The precedents locate where questions come from. In points and territory games the score sits on the board, so position matters every turn: Riftbound scores both conquering and holding, Smash Up pays three places at each base, and Frank Karsten advises Lorcana players that early board control matters more than racing to 20 lore ([Riot: How to Play](https://playriftbound.com/en-us/news/rules-and-releases/how-to-play-get-started/); [AEG Smash Up rulebook](https://www.alderac.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/SU7_Rulebook.pdf); [Ultimate Guard](https://ultimateguard.com/en/blog/lorcana-beginners-guide-tips-frank-karsten)). Mark Rosewater lists a catch-up feature and inertia, a game's push toward its own end, among the things every game needs ([Making Magic](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/ten-things-every-game-needs-part-1-2011-10-24)). The clocks are explicit: Snap doubles the stakes on its last turn ([Wikipedia: Marvel Snap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvel_Snap)), Dune: Imperium's conflict deck escalates through three levels across ten rounds (secondary source) ([Meadow Party](https://meadowparty.com/blog/2024/10/22/dune-imperium/)), and Deadlock itself caps kill-bounty growth at 40 minutes and, since March 2026, shortens the wave gap at 35 minutes to push games to a finish ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/deadlock/update-march-2026-patch-notes)). Nearly every timed neutral reward in the survey is announced in advance, from TFT's PvE rounds to Underlords' item rounds (moved from every five rounds to every four in 2020) and Battlegrounds' trinkets on turns 6 and 9 ([Underlords Wiki](https://dotaunderlords.fandom.com/wiki/February_25,_2020_Patch); [HearthstoneTopDecks](https://www.hearthstonetopdecks.com/developer-insights-battlegrounds-trinkets/)); the exception, Rum & Bones' Kraken, arrives on a die roll that grows likelier as the game runs ([SAHM Reviews](https://www.sahmreviews.com/2016/01/cool-mini-or-not-rum-and-bones.html)). No surveyed two-player card game has a recurring neutral boss both players fight over, so §3.4's mid-boss has no direct precedent to borrow numbers from.

**Inference.** The §3.3 spine (income, shop, act, end) supplies earn and buy; spike, fight and objective need three more pieces. First, a question on turns without a boss: with bosses on engine turns 5 and 10 and a median game of 14, most turns ask nothing positional, and a point for holding the lane (Riftbound) or an income choice such as the Active farming two souls instead of attacking puts one on every turn (Faeria already offers a resource, a card or land each turn ([Wikipedia: Faeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faeria))). Second, a score that visibly climbs: counting kills and boss points up toward a target, with each hero's bounty printed on its card the way Pokémon prints prize value, turns "a patron nobody attacks" into a race both sides watch. Third, a clock: an economy lengthens games because both sides keep buying, so the shop should ship with an escalation (bounties or patron losses that grow after a set turn, or a final boss that decides the game) and an on-screen countdown.

### Unfair cards: tempo is the mispriced unit, and the first mover cashes it

The sim's clearest outliers are tempo heroes: Haze wins **71.7%** of 127 appearances and Lady Geist **64.4%** of 132 (`sim-reports/latest.md`), and most of §2's unfair examples are extra or denied actions (Haze's free second swing, Burst Fire's stacking extra attack, Curse's three turns of shutdown). The precedents price exactly these effects high. In the one published per-effect regression on a commercial card game, Hearthstone's Windfury (a second attack) cost **1.19 mana per attack point on top of the attack's own 0.57**, hard removal 5.33 and a freeze 1.02, against 0.82 for a point of damage and **0.34 for a point of healing** ([Bursztein](https://elie.net/blog/hearthstone/how-to-find-automatically-hearthstone-undervalued-cards)). Games that measured seat edges found that fast, aggressive metas widened them, to 6 points in Magic's fastest limited format and 62% in Hearthstone's Odd Paladin mirror ([MTGAZone](https://mtgazone.com/?p=141724); [Vicious Syndicate](https://www.vicioussyndicate.com/?p=14794)). Designers blunt burst with structure: Mercenaries starts every ability that has a cooldown unavailable ([Hearthstone Wiki: Mercenaries](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Mercenaries)), Pokémon ends the turn when a Pokémon attacks ([Wikipedia: Pokémon TCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Trading_Card_Game)), and Sorcery's avatar at zero life survives the rest of the turn and needs a separate death blow *(excerpt)* ([PHD Games](https://www.phdgames.com/2023/07/18/sorcery-contested-realm-tcg-eriks-curiosa/)). For reading results, Riot nerfs a League champion that is overpowered for any audience and buffs one only if it is weak for all of them ([League of Legends](https://www.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/dev/dev-balance-framework-update/)), Hearthstone weighs play rate alongside win rate *(excerpt)* ([Icy Veins](https://icy-veins.com/forums/topic/35718-decoding-blizzards-policy-on-nerfs)), and Riftbound's first bans hit cards that were acceptable alone but too strong together *(excerpt)* ([Riftbound](https://riftbound.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/announcements/announcing-riftbounds-first-bans)).

**Inference.** Haze's passive is Windfury given away, and in a game of 4–7-HP heroes where one side always swings first, free tempo turns straight into seat advantage; that is why the hero outliers and the 66% share a cause. The order of work follows: price extra attacks, removal and hard crowd control on the Active above the flat budget and heals and shields below it (section 3); make any extra attack an explicit exception to a rule that one attack ends the Act step; start bought actives on cooldown; and give hard crowd control charges rather than an endless cooldown. Then judge heroes by Riot's asymmetric rule, log draft pick order (a hero always taken first is a problem even at 55%), and test hero-and-item pairs, not items alone. Ultimates that cost anywhere from 2 to 10 souls could follow Star Wars: Unlimited's leaders, which deploy once a player controls a threshold of 4 to 7 resources *(excerpt)* ([SWU Quickstart](https://cdn.xzone.sk/docs/sw_unlimited_quickstart_rules.pdf)).

### Builds that never execute: today's deck delivers a three-item plan about one game in four

The current rules make the failure measurable. Decks are **15 cards** with at most **2 copies**, the opening hand is **3** and each turn draws **1** ([playerData.ts](../src/storage/playerData.ts); [constants.ts](../src/engine/constants.ts)), and the median game lasts **14 engine turns, or 7 own turns** (`sim-reports/latest.md`). Computed from those constants, ignoring the mulligan:

| By own turn | Cards seen | All three items of a plan, one copy each | At least one of each, two copies each |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 7 of 15 | 7.7% | 34.8% |
| 6 | 9 of 15 | 18.5% | 60.4% |
| 7 (median game) | 10 of 15 | 26.4% | 72.5% |
| 10 | 13 of 15 | 62.9% | 97.1% |

The precedents converge on removing every cause. Artifact keeps items on a dead hero ([PCGamesN: How to play Artifact](https://www.pcgamesn.com/artifact/how-to-play-artifact)), Cloudspire's returning heroes keep upgrades and equipment ([Chip Theory Games](https://support.chiptheorygames.com/support/solutions/articles/33000244112-when-a-hero-returns-to-its-fortress-does-it-keep-its-equipment-and-upgrades-)), and no surveyed game strips gear on death, so today's rule is the outlier. Flesh and Blood, Grand Archive and Riftbound guarantee a plan's spine by keeping it out of the draw (section 1). Artifact's launch shop still drew a player's own item deck one card at a time, and Valve's 2.0 shop made about half of all offers come from the player's own list, up from a third ([Devtrackers](https://devtrackers.gg/artifact/p/7ecc43bf-let-s-shop-continued)). Mercenaries fixed equipment before the match, which executes every time but leaves nothing to decide during it ([Hearthstone Wiki: Mercenaries](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Mercenaries)). Deadlock itself discounts an upgrade by the cost of the component it consumes, so an ordered build never wastes an early purchase ([Deadlock Wiki: Item](https://deadlock.wiki/Item)).

**Inference.** The redesign removes all three causes in §2 (a deck blind to the draft, draw order, items lost on death) and matches the consensus. Two risks remain. Time: at 5–7 own turns, with skills, actives and retreats spending the same 4 souls a turn, a four-to-six-item build can still go unfinished (section 3). Offer luck: shop model B reintroduces the draw problem, and as written it delivers a specific item no more reliably than today's deck (section 4). Component discounts and a same-turn full refund are cheap insurance for the ordered build.

### Not Deadlock: reviewers credit objectives, spikes and comebacks, and the shop supplies the Deadlock part

Section 1 shows what made translations read as MOBAs. A champion-centric game can still miss: Riftbound's executive producer said in 2026 that Legends of Runeterra never really felt about its champions, despite their level-up flips ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/riftbound-producer-legends-of-runeterra-failed-lack-champion-focus/); [Riot Support](https://support.riotgames.com/legends-of-runeterra/gameplay/champion-basics)). This game's levels add +1 HP, power and spirit each, cap at level 4 after steps of 5, 7 and 9 experience, and grant the ultimate at level 2 ([constants.ts](../src/engine/constants.ts); §1). Deadlock offers native mechanics the plan has not used: category investment bonuses that jump at 4,800 souls spent in one category (weapon damage from +18% to +46%) and are lost when the items are sold ([Deadlock Wiki: Item](https://deadlock.wiki/Item)); flex slots unlocked by destroying Walkers ([Deadlock Wiki: Extra Slots](https://deadlock.wiki/Extra_Slots)); Rejuvenator crystals either team can steal ([Deadlock Wiki: Mid-Boss](https://deadlock.wiki/Mid-Boss)); and unsecured souls that a hero loses on death until they convert to secured ones ([Deadlock Wiki: Souls](https://deadlock.wiki/Souls)).

**Inference.** Measured against what reviewers credited, the plan delivers kill gold turned into items and one objective, and it lacks a score that counts up, a visible spike beyond the ultimate, a lane that can be pushed, and comebacks that must be taken rather than handed out. The cheapest Deadlock-native fills are a 1 : 2 : 4 : 8 ladder with component discounts; a category spike (+1 to a stat line once a hero's items in one tab reach a threshold); a fourth slot unlocked by the mid-boss; the Rejuvenator as stealable revive insurance; souls earned this turn that are lost if your Active dies before your next turn; a level threshold that visibly flips the hero card; and Guards of Atlantis's choose-one-of-two upgrade for §3.7. Every one of these stays deterministic and resolves in one step, which is what Cloudspire's and Artifact's reviewers asked for.

### The flat budget charges more than twice the market rate for heals and shields

§3.6 prices one soul at about one damage, one heal, one shield, one turn of one hero's stat or half a turn of hard crowd control, with a permanent stat point at two. Elie Bursztein's 2014 least-squares fit of 134 Hearthstone cards gives a different exchange rate ([Bursztein](https://elie.net/blog/hearthstone/how-to-find-automatically-hearthstone-undervalued-cards); [method](https://elie.net/blog/hearthstone/how-to-appraise-hearthstone-card-values)):

| Effect | §3.6 price | Hearthstone, mana per point | Relative to one damage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Damage | 1 soul | 0.82 | 1.00 |
| Heal | 1 soul | 0.34 | 0.41 |
| Health on a body (nearest to a shield) | 1 soul per shield point | 0.40 | 0.49 |
| Attack on a body | 2 per permanent point | 0.57 | 0.70 |
| Freeze (one target loses one attack) | 2 per turn of hard CC | 1.02 | 1.24 |
| Windfury (a second attack) | free on Haze | 1.19 per attack point, added to 0.57 | 1.45 |
| Draw a card | none | 1.84 | 2.24 |
| Destroy a minion | none | 5.33 | 6.50 |

The fit has known limits: it ignores board state, its author concedes some odd results, it reports no error term, and a separate open-source predictor gets Hearthstone costs exactly right only about half the time *(excerpt)* ([npm: hearthstone-card-cost](https://npmjs.org/package/hearthstone-card-cost)). Hearthstone heroes also have 30 health against this game's 4–7 ([Hearthstone Wiki: Hero](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Hero)). The one card game that runs on a single interchangeable budget, Flesh and Blood, can do so because a point of block cancels exactly a point of attack ([Star City Games](https://articles.starcitygames.com/flesh-and-blood/how-to-play-flesh-and-blood-2/)). Richard Garfield advises tuning through one or a few cost numbers while admitting there is no formula ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/magic-the-gathering-s-richard-garfield-s-strategies-for-game-balancing)), and Hearthstone's Ben Brode is quoted as saying card balance has no 1% changes *(excerpt)* ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/there-are-no-one-percent-changes-ben-brode-on-how-hearthstone-balance-differs-from-overwatch)).

**Inference.** Four corrections follow. Heals and shields are worth 0.4 to 0.5 of a damage point, so at one soul each they are overpriced next to damage, which pushes every build toward damage and tempo. Crowd control should be priced by what it denies: a stun on Haze's Active removes up to 6 damage, a stun on a bench hero removes one skill, and Curse (three turns for 5) against Knockdown (one turn for 5) is a threefold rate gap under any budget. Permanent stats should be priced by expected uses: because items now survive death, +1 attack worn by the Active from own turn 2 of a 7-turn match adds up to about 5 damage, two and a half times its price at one soul a damage. Repeatables need a multiplier: cooldowns count the owner's turns ([turn.ts](../src/engine/actions/turn.ts)), so in a 5–7-turn match a cooldown-2 active bought on own turn 2 fires about three times and a cooldown-3 active about twice. And at T1 = 3 souls one soul is a 33% step, so price internally on a finer grain and round for display. With 93 items, a linear model refit to sim results each pass is the right size of tool; machine learning is not needed.

### The 300-game sim cannot resolve its own gates

The sim ran **300 random-draft games in 14.5 seconds**; the first player won 198, and each of 19 heroes appeared 110 to 144 times (`sim-reports/latest.md`; [balance-sim.ts](../scripts/balance-sim.ts)). Standard binomial intervals give:

| §3.6 gate | What 300 games resolve | False alarm rate if the truth is exactly fair | Games the gate needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| First player 50 ± 3 | ±5.7-point 95% interval | about 30% | about 1,070 for a ±3 interval; about 2,200 to tell a true 53% from 50% with 80% power |
| Every hero 45–55 | about 126 appearances, ±8.7 points | about 26% per hero, roughly five of 19 | about 912 (384 appearances) at 95%; about 1,580 at 99%, which cuts the chance of any false flag across 19 heroes from about 62% to about 17% |
| Item win rate when bought, 45–55 | depends on how often the item is bought | not defined | about 192 ÷ f games for 384 purchases at buy rate f (about 1,900 at 10%, 6,400 at 3%) |

Applied to today's report, only two hero flags are statistically clear: Haze (95% interval about 64–80%) and Lady Geist (about 56–73%) survive a correction for testing 19 heroes, Lash (31–49%) and Seven (33–49.5%) are suggestive, and Yamato, Wraith, Viscous and Abrams are consistent with noise. The first player's 66% (interval about 61–71%) is unambiguous. On method, the literature is consistent but reached the researchers secondhand: restricted-play experiments, in which an AI barred from one option plays an unrestricted one, measure an option's value without the bias that winners buy more *(excerpt)* ([Jaffe et al., AIIDE 2012](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12513)), and an evolutionary search over card numbers has pushed Hearthstone matchups toward 50% while changing as few cards as possible *(excerpt)* ([arXiv 1907.01623](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01623v1)).

**Inference.** At about 50 milliseconds a game, 2,500 games cost two minutes, so the gates' real requirement is discipline, not compute: play each sampled draft twice with seats swapped, report seat and hero results from at least 2,500 games, fit hero and item effects jointly by logistic regression (a four-hero team's result credits all four), run restricted-play arms for the most-bought items, and run two AI strengths, nerfing what is strong under either and buffing only what is weak under both. The shop will slow each game, but not enough to change that conclusion.

### The plan mixes two kinds of turn, which halves every budget in it

The engine's match turn counts both players' turns. `beginTurn` sets `G.turnNumber` from boardgame.io's turn counter minus the draft offset, the first player takes the odd turns, and the soul refill indexes a player's own turn as (turn − 1) ÷ 2 rounded down ([turn.ts](../src/engine/actions/turn.ts)). The sim's "13 turns mean, 5 to 23" is in those units (`sim-reports/latest.md`). Respawn, by contrast, counts the owner's turns (`RESPAWN_TURNS`, per the comment beside the `tickRespawn` call), skill cooldowns count the owner's turns, and §3.1's income arrives at the start of each player's own turn ([turn.ts](../src/engine/actions/turn.ts); [constants.ts](../src/engine/constants.ts)).

| Match length (engine turns) | Own turns each | Lane souls each at 4 a turn | Left for the shop if two 1-soul skills are cast a turn | What that remainder buys at 3/6/11/18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 (gate floor) | 5 | 20 | 10 | one T2 and one T1 |
| 13 (today's mean) | 6–7 | 24–28 | 12–14 | one T3 and at most one T1, or two T2 |
| 14 (gate ceiling) | 7 | 28 | 14 | one T3 and one T1, or two T2 |
| 20 | 10 | 40 | 20 | one T3, one T2 and one T1 |

Kill bounties (2 to 5 souls at levels 1–4) and the boss's 4 add to these totals.

**Inference.** Under §3.6's own gate, each player gets 5–7 turns, so §3.2's "a T4 the late game" has almost no late game to live in: 18 souls is 64–90% of a player's lane income for the entire match. The budgets in the research notes, which assumed 10–14 own turns, should be halved. Skill costs interact with this directly: with four heroes each able to cast a one-soul skill, skills alone can absorb the whole 4-soul lane income, so saving would mean not using kits. The fix is an order of decisions: set match length in own turns first, decide how many purchases a match should hold, and only then set income and the ladder, with every rule stating which kind of turn it counts.

### The mid-boss opens on the first player's turn and pays only the last hit

Under the engine's numbering, turn 5 is the first player's third turn, turn 10 the second player's fifth and turn 15 the first player's eighth ([turn.ts](../src/engine/actions/turn.ts)), and the turn-15 boss arrives after today's median game has ended (`sim-reports/latest.md`). At turn 5 the first player has collected three incomes to the second player's two. §3.4's boss has 12 HP and hits back for 2, while §2 describes 2- and 3-power heroes (Haze's double swing reaches 6 a turn), so the boss takes several swings, and only the side that lands the kill is paid. Riftbound has met the seat half of this problem: Riot's July 2026 note singled out a battlefield that gave each player a point at their first beginning phase as particularly strong for the player going first ([GamerTag Mythras](https://gamertagmythras.com/blog/riftbound-tcg/riftbound-going-second-rules-test)). Precedents for paying by contribution or by deficit are plentiful: Smash Up pays the top three players at each base ([AEG Smash Up rulebook](https://www.alderac.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/SU7_Rulebook.pdf)), Dune: Imperium pays each conflict by strength rank with tied players taking the next reward (secondary source) ([Meadow Party](https://meadowparty.com/blog/2024/10/22/dune-imperium/)), TFT's carousel lets the lowest-health players pick first ([LoL Wiki: TFT Carousel](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/TFT:Carousel)), League's objective bounties pay only the team that is behind, scaled to 10% of its gold deficit and capped at 1,000 ([League Wiki: Objective bounties](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/Objective_bounties)), and Deadlock lets either team steal the Rejuvenator crystals ([Deadlock Wiki: Mid-Boss](https://deadlock.wiki/Mid-Boss)).

**Inference.** Last-hit-takes-all on a multi-swing boss invites a standoff in which neither side wants to soften it for the other, and it rewards whoever can burst it, usually the side already ahead. Three fixes have precedent: spawn the boss at the start of a round and pay the side that dealt more damage to it over that round, with ties going to the side behind on net worth; give the side behind first access; or let crystals be stolen, as Deadlock does. The cadence matters as well: a schedule on odd engine turns alternates seats, while an all-even schedule such as 4/8/12 gives every opening to the second player, which could serve as deliberate compensation once the payout is fixed. §3.4 should also state whether the boss persists until killed, because a boss present for one turn only is a gift to whoever's turn it is.

### A souls head start did not fix Riftbound, and today's engine would erase it

Riot gave Riftbound's second player a third rune on its first turn, a lead that lasts five turns because the rune deck is capped at twelve ([GamerTag Mythras](https://gamertagmythras.com/blog/riftbound-tcg/riftbound-going-second-rules-test)), and in October 2026 still found going first above its target; it is now testing compensation in the win currency itself (a starting point), an extra card or a prediction, with no percentages published ([Riot](https://playriftbound.com/en-us/news/organizedplay/testing-start-of-game-rules/)). Hearthstone needed the Coin plus a fourth card to reach about 52%, and found that a life bonus moved the numbers without fixing the feeling of always having less to spend ([BlizzPro](https://blizzpro.com/?p=70449)). Gwent shows the opposite risk, a second seat advantaged by having the last word ([TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/gwent-the-witcher-card-game-review)). This repository adds two facts. The refill overwrites the soul pool at the start of every turn (`ps.souls = soulRefillForTurn(realTurn)`), so a starting balance given at setup is erased before the second player can spend it ([turn.ts](../src/engine/actions/turn.ts)). And the edges stack: `DRAFT_ORDER` gives seat 0 the first pick, the step that ends the draft starts seat 0's turn ([setup.ts](../src/engine/actions/setup.ts)), and the board hands seat 1 to the AI ([Board.tsx](../src/ui/Board.tsx)), so in a normal match the human always drafts first and moves first. The sim's random drafts measure the seat alone.

**Inference.** On the measure Hearthstone and 17Lands use, the gap between the two seats' win rates, the sim's 66% is a 32-point gap, against 1 to 5.75 points for Hearthstone after the Coin and about 3 for Magic limited, so no single gift is likely to close it. Step 0's number is right for the gold economy: with flat income I and alternating turns, a head start of I/2 (2 souls at 4 a turn) zeroes the average income gap. It is the wrong tool for the current refill game, where the first player's spending lead grows by a soul each round (Brode's 28 to 21), and in the current engine it must be applied as a bonus on top of the second player's first refill or it does nothing. In either economy it leaves first swing, first bounty and first boss access untouched, which is what the Riftbound and Hearthstone records predict. Test it as one sim arm among several: the head start; the first player's Active unable to attack on turn 1 (Pokémon, Yu-Gi-Oh); actives and skills that start on cooldown (Mercenaries); a one-point patron or score head start (Riftbound's test, Gwent's boost); burst repricing; then the best combination. Because the human always holds seat 0, every seat fix will make quick matches harder, so give AI difficulty its own dial rather than leaving the seat edge as a hidden handicap. A draft pie rule (the first drafter moves second, or the second player chooses a seat after the first pick) is the classic equalizer for stacked order *(excerpt)* ([Wikipedia: Pie rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pie_rule)).

## 4. Shop models: A beats B, and a market fixed before turn one beats both

§5 frames the choice as certainty against texture: A puts the whole catalogue on sale every turn with builds as a guide; B offers five items a turn from the tiers a player can afford, with a one-soul reroll and one lock. The precedents line up more clearly here than on any other question in this report, and they point past both options.

The case against B starts with Dominion. Donald X. Vaccarino first imagined a row of cards refilled after each purchase and rejected it because "too much of the game would rest on" a good card turning up just before a player's turn; he put every card on the table and kept variety by changing the ten kingdom piles between games ([Secret History of Dominion](https://dominionstrategy.com/2013/06/24/the-secret-history-of-dominion/)). The market deckbuilders that did adopt a random row (Ascension's six cards, Star Realms' five, Hero Realms' five, Clank!'s six, Dune: Imperium's five) each set it beside an always-available reserve, so a bad row never leaves a player nothing worth buying ([RulesPal: Ascension](https://rulespal.com/ascension/rulebook); [Wikipedia: Star Realms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Realms); [RulesPal: Hero Realms](https://rulespal.com/hero-realms/rulebook); [RulesPal: Clank!](https://rulespal.com/clank/rulebook); [RulesPal: Dune: Imperium](https://rulespal.com/dune-imperium/rulebook)). Artifact, the only major card game with a Dota-style shop, launched with three slots: a random Secret Shop item from the whole catalogue (one gold to hold it for a round), the top card of the player's own item deck, and a random consumable ([SteelSeries](https://steelseries.com/en-si/blog/artifact-blog-77)). Valve reportedly rebuilt it because getting the strongest items depended too much on luck *(excerpt)* ([Dot Esports](https://dotesports.com/general/news/valve-clarifies-artifacts-shopping-system-reveals-more-reworks)). The 2.0 shop drew every slot from the player's own ten items topped up with eleven random ones spread across five tiers, so **about half of all offers came from the player's own list, up from a third**; it gated offers by a shop level the player paid to raise, and it **removed the reroll button**, refilling a slot on purchase instead ([Devtrackers: Let's Shop! (continued)](https://devtrackers.gg/artifact/p/7ecc43bf-let-s-shop-continued)). Neither version saved the game, and contemporary accounts blame monetization and complexity rather than the shop ([Wikipedia: Artifact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_(video_game))). The one controlled experiment, a purpose-built card game tested under four combinations of input and output randomness, found that **input randomness significantly lowered satisfaction**; only its abstract was read, so its sample and effect sizes are unknown ([arXiv 2107.08437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08437)). Soren Johnson's rule of thumb is that luck feels fairer the earlier it acts and unfair when it decides a game late ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/analysis-soren-johnson-on-playing-the-odds)).

**Inference.** B's offers are input randomness, the kind Keith Burgun's distinction tolerates ([keithburgun.net](https://keithburgun.net/riot-is-thinking-about-inputoutput-randomness-in-league-of-legends)), but they spread luck across the whole match, and unless they favor the hero's build they deliver a specific item no better than the deck B replaces. The catalogue has **93 items**, 56 equipment cards and 37 spells that would become actives ([equipment.ts](../src/cards/equipment.ts); [spells.ts](../src/cards/spells.ts)). With all of them eligible, a given item is offered 5.4% of the time per shop, **28% of the time across six shops and 48% with a reroll every turn**; with only a third of them eligible, about 65% across six shops. Today's deck shows a single-copy card by own turn 7 in 67% of games, and a reroll every turn costs six souls a match, the price of a T2. B also turns "save for a T3" into saving for whichever T3 appears, and random offers get harder to plan as tiers rise: in Battlegrounds, assuming uniform draws from a full pool, the chance that a full shop shows at least one minion of the player's top tier falls from about 98% at tier 2 to about 41% at tier 6. The auto-battler support for B is weaker than it looks, since TFT and Battlegrounds spread shop luck across eight players and add freezes, odds, finite pools and tier gates, while in a duel every offer bears on the one opponent.

A has the strongest MOBA precedent of the three: Deadlock sells all **156** of its purchasable items in its shop ([Deadlock Wiki: Item](https://deadlock.wiki/Item)), and A is the answer Dominion's designer reached. Prismata is its card-game test, and even Prismata offers a random subset of units each game while paying the full balancing cost (section 1). Lunarch's advisers warned that luckless games drive weaker players away, and Grant found that beginners felt unlucky anyway, because their own choices were effectively random ([Game Developer: The role of luck](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-role-of-luck-why-rng-isn-t-the-answer)). Garfield's "Getting Lucky" argues that luck widens the range of opponents who can enjoy a game, lets losers blame chance and varies play; in his tank example, random prices or availability stop players believing one unit is always best. He adds that matchmaking reduces the need for luck as an equalizer and that heavy luck suits only short games ([Garfield, Getting Lucky](http://www.superdan.net/download/blog/deltasdnd/Garfield_GettingLucky_GDM_November_2006.pdf)). Brode's measure of a good game is the density of decisions and how fun they are, not their number ([Think Like a Game Designer #42](https://justingarydesign.substack.com/p/think-like-a-game-designer-42-ben-e6a)).

**Inference.** Within a match A is the only option that guarantees execution, and because this game's opponent is always the AI, difficulty settings do the equalizing Garfield assigns to matchmaking, so A's lack of luck costs less here than it did Prismata. A's real costs are two: every mispriced item becomes something both players must buy (Burst Fire today), and a 93-item menu across four heroes is held to a manageable decision only by the lit build item.

The design the evidence converges on is a third: **C, a market fixed before turn one**. Every source above agrees on one sequencing rule (spend luck before the match, keep play deterministic, show everything), and Prismata's base-plus-random set and Dominion's ten piles implement it. Artifact 2.0 moved toward it with the player's own list plus tiered random supplements, and Hearthstone's Arena keeps offers fair by matching the power of each pick and by packaging a legendary with three cards that support it ([Blizzard](https://hearthstone.blizzard.com/en-us/news/21591736); [Blizzard Watch](https://blizzardwatch.com/2025/05/30/hearthstone-revamps-arena/)).

**Inference.** As a concrete proposal, C sells every T1 plus each drafted hero's signature items for both sides at all times, adds a random slice of the remaining catalogue stratified by tab and tier, reveals the market before the hero draft so drafting can respond to it, and fixes it for the match. Plans execute as in A, matches differ as in B, a broken item breaks only the matches it appears in, the AI and the sim face a much smaller item space, and the public snake draft gains something to draft against. The trap is the guarantee: if any item may sit in a build and every build item is guaranteed, C collapses into A for anything worth building around, so the guaranteed set should be T1 basics and hero signatures, and the riskiest effects (stacking extra attacks, hard crowd control) belong in the rotating slice, where Garfield's argument protects the game. Dominion shipped fixed first-game kingdoms because a random ten sometimes hands a newcomer the worst possible start ([Secret History of Dominion](https://dominionstrategy.com/2013/06/24/the-secret-history-of-dominion/)); the tutorial and story should use fixed markets for the same reason. A lighter variant, A+, keeps A and puts one random item on sale each turn at a discount, as Slay the Spire discounts one card per shop and Backpack Battles refunds sale items in full ([StS Wiki: Merchant](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Merchant); [Backpack Battles Wiki: Gold](https://backpackbattles.wiki.gg/wiki/Gold)): luck that can only help a plan. If B is kept anyway, every precedent's guardrail applies: a private row, at least two of the five offers from the hero's saved build, offers matched in power or one per tier, a free freeze as in Battlegrounds, rerolls that cost 1 and then 2 as in Backpack Battles, and an always-available reserve of T1s.

The research narrows these choices but cannot make them; each needs a target from the designer and a measurement from the sim or a phone playtest. They are listed in the order the earlier sections suggest settling them, since each one changes the prices set after it.

## Conclusion

The research changes the order of the work more than its direction. §6 puts the pricing pass fourth, after the economy exists, but three decisions silently invalidate any price set before them: how many of each player's turns a match lasts, which fixes how many purchases it can hold and therefore the ladder; how the first mover's tempo is capped, through first-turn limits, actives that start on cooldown and extra swings as explicit exceptions; and which seat opens each boss and how its reward is split. Prismata's dozen restarts show how expensive a deterministic economy is to balance even when its structure holds still; pricing before these three are settled invites the same churn. The repository adds two traps the plan could not see from the design side: step 0's head start disappears unless it is applied after the refill, and because the human always sits in the seat that wins two AI-versus-AI games in three, every fairness fix will read as the AI getting harder unless difficulty becomes a dial of its own.

The other shift is from asking how much luck the game should have to asking when luck should act. Grant's luckless Prismata, Vaccarino's table of piles, Garfield's defense of luck, Johnson's early-luck rule and Valve's retreat from random offers all fit luck spent before turn one and determinism after it, and none fits an in-match offer lottery in a two-player game whose core complaint is that plans fail. A market fixed before the match is therefore not a compromise between A and B but the design both bodies of evidence describe. Running the sim at the scale its gates need, about 2,500 seat-mirrored games a pass or two minutes today, is the cheapest change this report recommends, and it is the one that makes every other recommendation testable.
